Axolotl Anatomy: Gills, Lungs, Teeth & Body Parts

Axolotl anatomy includes external gills, simple lungs, permeable skin, small oral teeth, lidless eyes, an inner ear and lateral-line system, olfactory organs, and a tongue with taste buds. This guide explains how those structures work without splitting closely related anatomy questions into separate pages.

Do axolotls have teeth?

Yes. Axolotls have real teeth in the mouth and palate, even though the teeth are small and easy to miss. Research on Ambystoma mexicanum describes organized outer and inner dental arcades and multiple tooth fields rather than a toothless mouth.

Studies of axolotl dentition also show that replacement teeth continue to develop. The teeth become more numerous as the animal develops, and their structure changes with developmental stage.

For owners, the practical point is simple: a smooth-looking axolotl mouth does not mean the animal has no teeth. Questions about whether a nip can hurt belong in the axolotls and children safety guide; this page covers the anatomy itself.

Do axolotls have lungs?

Yes, axolotls have lungs, plus external gills and oxygen-absorbing skin. They breathe through all three systems, with the feathery external gills and the skin doing most of the work underwater. The lungs are simple sacs used for surface gulps, which makes the axolotl one of the most flexible breathers among amphibians.

A true amphibian built for a life spent entirely underwater keeps this gill-lung-skin combo its whole life.

How do axolotls breathe?

Axolotls breathe through three systems at once: external gills, skin, and lungs. Each system has a specific job:

SystemWhat it does
External gillsThree feathery gills per side extract oxygen from water
SkinAbsorbs oxygen directly through the moist skin
LungsSimple sacs gulp air at the surface
Gill flickingMoves water over the gill filaments to boost oxygen uptake
RegenerationGills regrow if damaged or nipped

The gills and skin handle the routine oxygen supply, and the lungs supplement them when the animal surfaces.

How many gills does an axolotl have?

An axolotl has six external gill stalks, three on each side of the head. Each stalk carries fine filaments that increase surface area for gas exchange.

Why are axolotl gills so large?

The gills are large because they provide substantial surface area for underwater gas exchange. Their feathery filaments expose blood-rich tissue to moving water. Gill condition can also change with stress and water quality, so persistent changes should be interpreted alongside husbandry conditions rather than on appearance alone.

Why does my axolotl go to the surface for air?

surface gulping can be normal because axolotls have lungs. Occasional trips to the surface are not automatically a problem. Frequent gulping or a sudden change should prompt a check of temperature, water quality, and aeration.

Can axolotls breathe out of water?

Axolotls are fully aquatic and should remain in water. Their external gills are adapted for underwater gas exchange and can collapse and dry when exposed to air. Lungs allow surface air gulps, but they do not make an axolotl a land animal.

What do healthy axolotl gills look like?

Healthy gills are typically full and feathery, but normal appearance varies between individuals. Persistent shrinking, loss of filaments, marked curling, or a sudden change should prompt a check of water quality, temperature, and other stressors rather than a diagnosis from gill shape alone.

What is buccal pumping?

Buccal pumping is rhythmic movement of the mouth and throat region that helps move water across respiratory surfaces. It is one part of how aquatic amphibians maintain gas exchange while resting underwater.

Do axolotls have lungs or gills?

Axolotls have both lungs and gills, plus skin respiration. The gills and skin dominate underwater, while the lungs allow occasional surface air gulps. All three systems are part of normal axolotl respiratory anatomy.

What happens to axolotl gills in poor water?

Poor water quality can damage or reduce the condition of external gills. Ammonia and nitrite are especially concerning because they injure delicate aquatic tissues. If the gills change noticeably, test the water first and correct the underlying husbandry problem rather than treating the gills as a diagnosis by themselves.

What other anatomy do axolotls have?

Several common anatomy questions are best answered together because they describe the same paedomorphic aquatic body plan.

Do axolotls have ears?

Axolotls have an inner ear, but no visible external ear flap. They also sense water movement through a lateral-line system of mechanoreceptive neuromasts. Research on Ambystoma mexicanum describes both inner-ear sensory hair cells and superficial lateral-line organs, so hearing and water-motion sensing are not limited to a visible outer ear.

Do axolotls have eyelids?

Normal paedomorphic axolotls do not develop movable eyelids. The American Museum of Natural History and San Diego Zoo both identify lidless eyes as one of the juvenile traits retained into adulthood.

Do axolotls have scales?

No. Axolotls do not have fish-like or reptile-like scales. Their skin is amphibian epidermis with mucus-producing cells and remains important for gas exchange. Histological studies describe specialized epidermal cells and a protective mucus layer rather than scales.

Do axolotls have a nose and a sense of smell?

Yes. Axolotls have external nostrils, a nasal cavity, an olfactory epithelium, and a vomeronasal system. Anatomical studies show that their olfactory receptor cells project to the olfactory bulb, and experiments have recorded odor responses from the olfactory epithelium.

Do axolotls have a tongue?

Yes. Axolotls have a tongue with specialized epithelium and taste buds. Microscopy studies describe taste buds on the tongue and elsewhere in the oral cavity, with sensory innervation through cranial nerves.

Axolotl anatomy combines structures that look unusual together: oral teeth, external gills, permeable skin, simple lungs, lidless eyes, aquatic sensory organs, and a taste-bearing tongue. Together they reflect the animal's permanently aquatic, paedomorphic salamander biology.
