Understanding Axolotl Pigment Cells

What Are the Three Axolotl Pigment Cells?

Axolotl skin color comes from three types of pigment cells, called chromatophores: melanophores produce black-brown pigment, xanthophores produce yellow-orange, and iridophores produce metallic shine.

Melanophores fill with eumelanin and create the dark areas of a wild type. Xanthophores carry yellow and orange pigments called pteridines and carotenoids, and iridophores contain thin reflective crystals that scatter light into a metallic sheen.

The three cell types sit in layers in the skin, and the color you see is the combination of all three layers viewed from above.

How Do Morphs Change the Pigment Cells?

Every color morph is the result of one or more pigment genes that deletes or reduces one of the three cell types, or changes the pigment a cell makes.

The table below maps the standard morphs to their pigment-cell changes:

MorphCell type changedResult
Wild typeNoneDark brown, olive, metallic
Leucistic (d/d)Melanophore migration blockedWhite body, dark eyes
Melanoid (m/m)Iridophores removedFlat black, no shine
Albino (a/a)Melanin production shut offWhite-yellow, pink eyes
CopperMelanin type shiftedRed-orange brown
Axanthic (ax/ax)Xanthophores removedGray, no yellow

The melanoid and axanthic genes are the strongest single-gene removals, deleting entire cell types. Albino and copper change what the cells make rather than whether they exist.

What Is an Axanthic Axolotl?

An axanthic axolotl carries the recessive ax/ax genotype, which removes xanthophores entirely so the animal shows no yellow or orange pigment.

An axanthic wild type appears gray or slate without the usual olive sheen, and an axanthic albino appears silvery-white instead of golden. The removal of yellow is often subtle, which is why axanthic is a rarer and less obvious morph.

Axanthic combines with melanoid and copper to produce the MAC (melanoid axanthic copper) morph, one of the darkest and most valuable axolotls.

Where Do Pigment Cells Come From?

All three pigment cell types develop from a shared population of embryonic cells called the neural crest, which migrates across the body early in development.

The neural crest gives rise to pigment cells, nerve cells, and cartilage cells. When a pigment gene such as leucism blocks the cells from migrating, patches of skin end up with no pigment at all.

Because every pigment cell shares this origin, a single gene change can delete a whole cell type across the entire animal, which is why morphs look uniform rather than patchy.

Do Pigment Cells Affect Axolotl Health?

No, losing any single pigment cell type does not harm axolotl health, growth, or lifespan, because the cells only carry pigment and have no immune or metabolic function.

Melanoid, albino, and axanthic axolotls eat, breed, and regenerate exactly like wild types. The only practical difference is visual: lighter morphs show wounds and stress more clearly.

Choose a morph on appearance and availability, because nothing about pigment cells changes a care routine. Start at the morphs hub and read the color genetics guide to see how the cell changes pass from parent to offspring.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pigment cells do axolotls have?

Axolotls have three pigment cell types: melanophores, xanthophores, and iridophores.

Which pigment cell makes axolotls shiny?

Iridophores make the metallic shine; their reflective crystals scatter light into a silver-blue sheen.

Do all axolotls have the same pigment cells?

No, every morph removes or changes one or more cell types, which is why colors differ.

What does axanthic mean in an axolotl?

Axanthic (ax/ax) removes xanthophores, so the animal shows no yellow or orange and appears gray or silvery.
