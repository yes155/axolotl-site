What Is a Wild Type Axolotl?

A wild type axolotl is the natural color form of Ambystoma mexicanum, carrying the dominant D allele at the white locus and all three pigment cell types. It looks dark brown or olive-green with black, yellow, and iridescent speckles across the body and gills.

Wild type is the baseline phenotype every other morph mutates from. Leucistic, golden albino, and melanoid axolotls carry recessive mutations at one or more pigment genes; a wild type carries none of those mutations as a homozygous pair.

A wild type axolotl costs $25 to $60, which makes it the most common and the least expensive morph sold by breeders.

What Color Is a Wild Type Axolotl?

A wild type axolotl is dark brown to olive-green, with black mottling, yellow patches, and a shiny gold ring around each pupil. Individual shade ranges from almost-black specimens to lighter tan animals, because pigment density varies between lines.

Three pigment cell types create the pattern. Melanophores produce the dark brown and black pigment, xanthophores add the yellow and olive tones, and iridophores produce the metallic speckle effect across the body and gills.

Yellow is strongest in juveniles and fades as the animal matures, while iridophores multiply as the axolotl approaches sexual maturity.

How Does a Wild Type Compare to Other Morphs?

The wild type is the only common morph that shows all three pigment cell types, because every other morph deletes or reduces at least one of them. Leucistic stops pigment-cell migration, golden albino removes melanophores, and melanoid removes iridophores.

What Is the Wild Type Axolotl Genotype?

The wild type axolotl genotype is D/- A/- M/- Ax/-, which means it is not homozygous for any of the four color mutations. The relevant genes are A (albino), M (melanoid), Ax (axanthic), and D (white locus).

The D allele drives the production of yellow xanthophores, which combine with melanophores to create the wild type's brown-olive base color. A dash stands for the second allele, which is either dominant or recessive without changing the phenotype.

A plain wild type cross produces 100% wild type offspring when neither parent carries a recessive color gene. Two wild types that both carry one albino allele (a) produce about 25% albino offspring.

How Do You Tell a Wild Type from a Melanoid?

The single reliable difference is the eye: a wild type has a shiny iridescent ring around the pupil, and a melanoid does not. Both animals appear dark brown or black at a glance.

The melanoid mutation (m/m) reduces xanthophores, removes iridophores entirely, and multiplies melanophores. The result is a uniformly dark animal with no metallic shine anywhere on the body, including the eyes.

A wild type shows the reflective eye ring even when its body is very dark. If no metallic ring surrounds the pupil under good light, the animal is a melanoid.

A wild type also keeps visible yellow speckling and a brown undertone, where a melanoid reads as flat black or dark gray.

Are Wild Type Axolotls Good Pets?

Yes, a wild type axolotl requires exactly the same care as every other morph, because color genetics do not affect health, temperament, or husbandry. Tank size, temperature, water quality, and diet are identical across all morphs.

Wild types are hardy and widely available from breeders, which makes them a sensible first axolotl. They eat the standard carnivore diet of earthworms, pellets, and frozen foods.

The only real difference is visual. A keeper who wants the classic natural look chooses wild type; a keeper who wants the darkest animal buys a melanoid instead.

How Does a Wild Type Change with Age?

A wild type's yellow tones fade and its iridescence strengthens as it matures, while the brown-olive base color stays constant. Juveniles show the yellow xanthophores most strongly, and adults show more metallic speckle across the body and gills.

The silhouette, gill size, and eye ring do not change with age. Only the balance of yellow versus shine shifts between the juvenile and adult stages.

Wild Type Variants and Color Intensity

"Wild type" is a range, not a single shade. Wild-type axolotls carry all three pigment-cell types — melanophores, xanthophores, and iridophores — and the balance of those cells decides whether an individual reads dark black-brown, olive, or a brighter "high-yellow" animal. This is the same three-cell mechanism covered in the pigment-cells guide, and it means no two wild types are exactly the same colour.

Rarity, Price, and Availability

The wild type is the baseline and one of the cheapest morphs to buy, usually from around $25. Because they are the natural form, they are widely available from reputable breeders and pet stores. Prices sit on the price-by-morph page; for buyer advice see breeder vs pet store.

The Wild Population Behind the Pet

Wild-type is the colour of the wild axolotl — but the wild population is critically endangered and survives only in the canals of Xochimilco near Mexico City. Pet wild types are captive-bred, never taken from the wild. For the animal's true situation, see the Xochimilco habitat and conservation status.
