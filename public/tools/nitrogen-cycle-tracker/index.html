Axolotl nitrogen cycle — frequently asked questions

How long does it take to cycle an axolotl tank?

Cycling an axolotl tank takes 4–8 weeks from scratch using pure ammonia dosing. With seeded filter media from an established tank, cycling can complete in 1–2 weeks.

Axolotl tanks cycle more slowly than tropical fish tanks because nitrifying bacteria colonise fastest at 77–86°F — but axolotl water must stay at 60–68°F. Budget 6 weeks before purchasing your axolotl.

How do I know when my axolotl tank is fully cycled?

Your axolotl tank is fully cycled when 2 ppm of ammonia drops to 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite within 24 hours, confirmed on two consecutive test days, with nitrate present. That is the only reliable completion criterion — this tracker flags it automatically.

Do a 50% water change to bring nitrate below 20 ppm before introducing your axolotl.

What should ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate read during cycling?

The expected progression during an axolotl nitrogen cycle:

  • Week 1–2: Ammonia rises and stays elevated. No nitrite yet.
  • Week 2–4: Nitrite appears and spikes high. Ammonia starts dropping. Both chemicals can be elevated simultaneously — this is normal and means both bacterial colonies are active.
  • Week 4–8: Nitrate appears and rises. Nitrite begins to fall. Ammonia approaches zero.
  • Cycle complete: Both ammonia and nitrite read 0 ppm. Nitrate is present. This state holds for two consecutive test days.
Can I add my axolotl while the tank is still cycling?

No. Never cycle a tank with an axolotl inside. Ammonia above 0.1 ppm — which is normal throughout active cycling — causes gill burns, neurological damage, and immune suppression within hours.

If you already have your axolotl and the tank is uncycled, keep the axolotl in a tub with 100% daily water changes using dechlorinated water while the tank completes its cycle.

Why is my axolotl nitrogen cycle stalling?

The most common cause of a stalled axolotl nitrogen cycle is pH dropping below 6.5, which slows nitrification, and below 6.0 halts it completely. Test your KH (carbonate hardness) — if it's below 3 dKH, the buffer is exhausted and pH is crashing. Add crushed coral or sodium bicarbonate to raise KH and stabilise pH above 7.0.

Other causes: chlorine in the water killing bacteria (always dechlorinate), water temperature too cold (below 60°F slows colonisation further), or nitrite spiking above 4–5 ppm (do a 50% water change to bring it down).

What test kit should I use to track my axolotl nitrogen cycle?

Use the API Freshwater Master Test Kit (liquid reagent, not strips). Paper test strips are not accurate enough to detect the early ammonia and nitrite spikes that matter most during cycling. The API kit tests ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and pH — all four parameters you need to track during the cycle.

What is the axolotl nitrogen cycle?

The nitrogen cycle is the biological process that makes a tank safe for an axolotl. Axolotl waste and uneaten food produce ammonia (NH₃), which is acutely toxic at any concentration above 0.1 ppm. Nitrifying bacteria — first Nitrosomonas, then Nitrobacter — colonise your filter media and convert ammonia to nitrite (NO₂) and finally to nitrate (NO₃). Nitrate is far less toxic and is managed with weekly 20–30% water changes.

Cycling establishes those bacterial colonies before the axolotl enters the water. An uncycled tank has no bacteria to process waste, so ammonia accumulates to lethal levels within days.

Axolotl water parameters — full reference

Once cycled, maintain: temperature 60–68°F (16–20°C), pH 7.4–7.6, ammonia 0 ppm, nitrite 0 ppm, nitrate under 20 ppm, GH 7–14 dGH, KH 3–8 dKH. Test weekly with a liquid kit. If ammonia or nitrite reads above 0, move your axolotl to a clean tub with daily water changes while you identify the cause.
