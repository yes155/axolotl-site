That permanent smile and feathery gill-crown make axolotls easy to fall for. But axolotls are not low-maintenance pets — they're cold-water amphibians with paper-thin skin and a long list of "common" advice that's actually dangerous. This guide cuts through that noise.

Quick Axolotl Care Overview

Axolotls need a 29–40 gallon tank, water kept between 59–64°F (15–18°C), weekly partial water changes, and a diet of earthworms or sinking pellets every 2–3 days as adults. They're solitary, intolerant of warm water, and can live 10–15 years with proper care.

**Care difficulty**Intermediate — manageable, but unforgiving of shortcuts
**Minimum tank size**20 gallons (29–40 gallons is the real safe minimum)
**Water temperature**59–64°F (15–18°C); never above 75°F
**Lifespan**10–15 years
**Diet**Carnivore — earthworms, nightcrawlers, sinking pellets
**Tank mates**None recommended — solitary species

What Exactly Is an Axolotl?

An axolotl is a salamander that never grows up — at least not on the outside. Most amphibians go through metamorphosis and move onto land; axolotls skip that step, staying aquatic and reaching full sexual maturity while still looking like a larva. Biologists call this neoteny.

The cause is one missing hormonal signal: the pituitary never tells the thyroid to release enough thyroxine, the hormone that triggers metamorphosis in other amphibians. Labs can force the change with hormone injections or high iodine doses, but it almost never happens on its own at home — and when it does, it usually signals hybridization with a tiger salamander or serious environmental stress, not a milestone.

That larval body also makes their skin unkeratinized and highly absorbent — useful in the wild, risky in a tank, because anything in the water gets into the animal, good or bad. Their skeleton is cartilage, not bone, which is part of why a hard substrate bump or rough handling does real damage.

How they breathe matters too. Axolotls breathe three ways at once — gills, skin, and small backup lungs — and watching which one they lean on is a real stress indicator. When oxygen runs low or they've been more active, they surface to gulp air; pulmonary breathing covers roughly 40% to 60% of their oxygen needs in those moments. If your axolotl is surfacing constantly, that's not curiosity. It usually means the water is short on dissolved oxygen, so check filtration and flow first.

Is an Axolotl Right for You?

Before you buy one, look at what you're actually signing up for. This isn't a "feed it once a week and forget it" pet.

The difficulty is real but specific. Axolotls aren't hard because they're fragile in some vague way — they're hard because the margin for error on water temperature and water chemistry is narrow, and the consequences of missing it are slow-building organ damage rather than an obvious crisis. If you're already comfortable running a cycled aquarium, axolotls are a manageable step up. If you've never kept fish before, expect a learning curve in your first few months.

What it actually costs. Setup typically runs $200–$600 upfront, plus $25–$40 a month, totaling roughly $3,800–$6,000 over a 12-year lifespan. The animal itself is cheap — $25–$90 for a common morph, $150–$400+ for rare ones like copper or chimera — the real cost is the equipment that keeps cold, clean water stable for over a decade. A chiller alone, if you need one, is often the single biggest line item.

Check the law before you check your wallet. Wild axolotls are Critically Endangered, with fewer than 1,000 individuals left in their native habitat, despite millions in captivity worldwide. That status has filtered into regulation:

  • Banned: California, Maine, New Jersey, Washington D.C., and (since 2024) Arkansas for new acquisitions
  • Permit required: New Mexico and Hawaii
  • Legal, no permit: Most other states, including Texas and Virginia (ban lifted 2021)

Laws shift. Check your specific state and city before you buy, not after.

Tank Setup & Habitat

What Size Tank Does an Axolotl Actually Need?

A single adult axolotl needs at least 20 gallons, though 29–40 gallons is the real safe minimum among experienced keepers. "10 gallons may suffice" is outdated advice that ignores how fast waste builds up in small volumes. More water means more dilution, more stable temperature, more margin for error.

Shape matters as much as volume: axolotls are weak-swimming bottom-dwellers, so floor space beats water depth. A long, low 40-gallon breeder beats a tall tank of the same volume.

A lid or tight-fitting hood matters more than people expect, too — axolotls can and do jump, usually when startled or when water quality has dropped enough to stress them.

What Substrate Is Safe for an Axolotl Tank?

The safest substrates are bare glass, fine sand under 1mm, or smooth slate — and standard aquarium gravel has no place in an axolotl tank. This comes down to how they eat: axolotls don't chew, they suck food in whole. Gravel matching their gape gets swallowed, can't pass the pyloric sphincter, and causes impaction that's often fatal without surgery.

  • Bare glass — safe and easy to clean, but no traction for the feet
  • Fine silica sand (under 1mm) — gold standard for axolotls over 6 inches; passes through the gut harmlessly if ingested. For a full breakdown of grain sizes and brands, see our guide to choosing the best axolotl substrate.
  • Smooth slate or tile — glass-level cleanliness with better footing
  • Large river rocks (bigger than the head) — safe from swallowing, but need regular siphoning underneath
  • Standard gravel or pebbles — banned; the leading cause of fatal intestinal blockages

Decor and Hiding Spots

Axolotls don't have eyelids and are sensitive to bright light, so hiding spots aren't optional decoration — they're how the animal manages stress. Caves, PVC tubes, or driftwood placed low in the tank all work. Keep decorations smooth; rough edges and sharp rock cut their skin easily, and a cut skin barrier is an open door for fungal infection.

Filtration

An axolotl needs a filter that turns over 3 to 6 times the tank's total volume per hour, with the output diffused so it never creates a strong current. Axolotls are weak swimmers with no tolerance for flow — a filter that's powerful but undiffused stresses them just as much as one that's too weak to keep the water clean.

  • Sponge filters — gentle and beginner-friendly, ideal for tanks under 40 gallons or for quarantine setups, though they offer less mechanical filtration
  • Hang-on-back filters — strong filtration for standard tanks, but need a baffle (a pre-filter sponge over the output, or a plant/driftwood placed in the current) to cut the flow
  • Canister filters — the best choice for tanks over 40 gallons, run quiet and out of sight, but need a spray bar to spread the output instead of blasting it in one direction

If your axolotl's gills are curling forward or it's swimming frantically near the filter outlet, that's a flow problem, not a water-quality one. Baffle the output before you change anything else.

Why the Nitrogen Cycle Matters So Much

The nitrogen cycle converts toxic ammonia into less-toxic nitrite, then into much-less-toxic nitrate — and your tank needs 4 to 8 weeks running this cycle before an axolotl goes in. Skipping it is the most common reason new axolotls die in their first month. For the exact step-by-step process, see our guide to cycling an axolotl tank fast.

  • Waste and uneaten food release ammonia
  • Nitrosomonas bacteria convert ammonia to nitrite
  • Nitrobacter bacteria convert nitrite to nitrate
  • Weekly water changes keep nitrate from creeping up anyway

These bacteria need carbonate hardness (KH) as a carbon source. Below 20 mg/L KH, the cycle stalls and ammonia piles up even in an "established" tank — test KH alongside ammonia and nitrite.

One quirk worth knowing: axolotls have a fully functional urea cycle, so they excrete urea as well as ammonia — an evolutionary holdover for surviving seasonal droughts that, in a tank, just means more nitrogenous waste to filter.

Water Parameters & Temperature

An axolotl needs water between 59–64°F (15–18°C), a pH of 7.4–7.6, zero detectable ammonia, zero nitrite, and nitrate under 10 mg/L. Each number maps to a specific, documented health failure — none of it is arbitrary. Even nitrate as low as 3 mg/L, sustained for four days, measurably cuts appetite and activity, which is why "a little nitrate is fine" is bad advice.

Temperature is the most underestimated parameter. Axolotls evolved in cool, spring-fed canals, and their metabolism is built for cold water. Push past the mid-70s°F and you accelerate their metabolic rate, suppress immunity, and invite fungal infections like Saprolegnia. Above 70°F room temperature, a real water chiller isn't optional — it's the most important piece of equipment in the setup. For model and sizing recommendations, see our guide to the best aquarium chillers for axolotls. Frozen bottles and clip-on fans cause swings that stress the animal more than steady warmth would.

Ammonia and nitrite stay at flat zero — no exceptions, regardless of what any other source claims. (One commonly cited veterinary care guide lists 1 ppm as "ideal" nitrite, which is wrong: at that level, nitrite oxidizes hemoglobin's iron until blood can't carry oxygen, a condition called methemoglobinemia — a fast track to gill necrosis and suffocation.)

Choosing and Sizing a Chiller

If your room temperature regularly sits above 70°F, you need one. Size it to your tank's total water volume, not the gallon number on the box alone — chillers are typically rated to drop a given volume by a set number of degrees, and undersizing means it runs constantly without ever catching up. In-line chillers paired with a canister filter are the quietest, most reliable setup for tanks 29 gallons and up; smaller nano chillers exist for smaller setups but struggle in warm rooms. Budget for this as part of the upfront cost, not an afterthought — see our chiller guide for specific models by tank size.

Water Changes & Conditioners

Weekly partial water changes of 20–30% are the baseline, even in a fully cycled tank, because nitrate accumulates regardless of how well-established your bacteria colony is.

Not every water conditioner is safe. Avoid anything containing aloe vera or other plant extracts — common in products marketed for "slime coat support" — because these ingredients are toxic to axolotls' permeable skin. Look for conditioners that strip chlorine and chloramine and detoxify ammonia without added botanicals.

Diet & Feeding

Axolotls are strict carnivores whose staple diet is earthworms or nightcrawlers, supplemented with high-protein sinking pellets. Nightcrawlers run over 60% protein with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio that protects against metabolic bone disease.

Life StageLengthFeeding FrequencyTypical Portion
HatchlingUnder 0.5 inTwice dailyAs much as eaten in 20–30 minutes
Juvenile3–6 inOnce daily1–2 pellets per inch, or a third of a nightcrawler
Subadult6–8 inEvery 1–2 daysOne nightcrawler or 5–8 pellets
AdultOver 8 inEvery 2–3 days1–2 nightcrawlers or 8–10 pellets

Avoid feeder goldfish and beef heart. Goldfish carry thiaminase, which destroys vitamin B1 and causes anemia, tremors, and loss of coordination over time, plus they're a common parasite source. Beef heart and other fatty meats overwhelm an axolotl's cold-water metabolism, causing fatty liver disease over time.

Pull uneaten food within 15–30 minutes — leftovers decompose into ammonia fast.

Tank Mates

Axolotls do best alone, and there's no real upside to changing that. They're solitary by nature — not stressed by isolation, not seeking company — and housing them together mostly adds risk: they regularly mistake tankmates for food and bite indiscriminately, especially around feeding time.

Fish are a particularly bad idea. Most species nip at an axolotl's feathery gills, mistaking them for food themselves, and any fish small enough to fit in an axolotl's mouth eventually will.

Snails are sometimes kept successfully once an axolotl is large enough not to swallow them, but introduce anything cautiously and quarantine it first — new arrivals are a common source of parasites and disease that an established tank otherwise wouldn't have.

If you do want to keep more than one axolotl, give each one effectively its own territory within a much larger tank (a 40-gallon breeder is the realistic floor for two), feed them at opposite ends to cut down on food-aggression nipping, and accept that accidental breeding is a real possibility if you end up with one of each sex.

How Do You Know If Your Axolotl Is Sick?

The fastest warning signs are changes in gill color, fecal appearance, and buoyancy. Axolotls can't tell you something's wrong, so their body does the talking.

  • White, cottony fuzz — fungal infection (Saprolegnia), usually from warm water or a mucus-layer scrape. Cool isolation water plus a diluted black tea bath is the standard first response — skip salt baths, which are a common but risky DIY fix that can burn the skin and disrupt thyroid function.
  • Pale or shrunken gills — ammonia or nitrite burns, not infection. If nitrite poisoning (methemoglobinemia) is suspected, a methylene blue bath is the actual antidote, not just clean water.
  • Persistent floating, tail tilted up — gut impaction, often from gravel or overfeeding. A 72-hour fast is the first step; if fridging is needed to slow things down, cap it at 3 weeks.
  • Bright green feces — prolonged starvation, not illness.
  • Red sores or hemorrhaging near limbs/vent — bacterial infection (Aeromonas/Red Leg Syndrome); needs a vet promptly.

If you must handle a sick axolotl, never touch one with dry hands. Wet your hands first, or use sterile water-based lubricant — dry skin strips their protective mucus layer instantly.

When none of this matches what you're seeing, or symptoms persist past a water change and a few days of observation, that's the point to involve an exotics-experienced vet rather than keep guessing.

Determining Sex

You generally can't sex an axolotl with any confidence until somewhere between 12 and 18 months old, and even then, it's not always obvious. Males develop a visibly swollen, protruding cloaca behind the back legs — that's the clearest tell. Females tend to stay flatter around the cloaca and often look proportionally rounder through the body, especially if they're carrying eggs, but body shape alone isn't reliable enough to bet on.

This matters mostly if you're housing more than one axolotl together: an unplanned male-female pairing in the same tank will breed, and you'll be dealing with hundreds of eggs you didn't plan for.

What's the Day-to-Day Care Routine for an Axolotl?

An axolotl's care routine breaks down into three cadences: daily spot checks, weekly testing and water changes, and monthly filter maintenance. Miss any one of these for long and water quality slips first.

Setup typically runs $200–$600 upfront, plus $25–$40 a month, totaling roughly $3,800–$6,000 over a 12-year lifespan. Frequently Asked Questions

Are axolotls fish?

No. Despite the nickname "Mexican walking fish," axolotls are salamanders — fully aquatic amphibians that never leave the water or develop the lungs-only breathing of land-dwelling relatives.

Can axolotls live with other axolotls?

Not recommended. They're solitary by nature, prone to biting each other (often mistaking gills or limbs for food), and a male-female pair housed together will breed, leaving you with eggs to manage.

How long do axolotls live?

Typically 10–15 years with proper cold-water care, though well-maintained tanks have produced individuals living past 17–20 years.

Can axolotls go on land?

No. They keep their gills and remain fully aquatic their entire lives, unlike other salamander species that undergo metamorphosis and move onto land.

What do axolotls eat?

Earthworms and nightcrawlers as the staple, supplemented with high-protein sinking pellets. Avoid feeder goldfish (thiaminase risk) and fatty meats like beef heart.

How big do axolotls get?

Most adults reach 9–12 inches, occasionally over a foot, generally settling into their full adult size by 12–18 months old.

Is it legal to own an axolotl in my state?

It depends. Ownership is banned in California, Maine, New Jersey, Washington D.C., and Arkansas (as of 2024); permitted with restrictions in New Mexico and Hawaii; and legal without a permit in most other states, including Texas and Virginia. Check your specific state and city before buying, since these laws do change.

The Bottom Line

Cold water, zero ammonia and nitrite, the right substrate, a real worm-and-pellet diet, and a chiller if your house runs warm — get those five things right and you've covered most of what actually kills pet axolotls. Everything else is detail work on top of that foundation.
