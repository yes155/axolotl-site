Axolotl Emergency Guide: First Aid & Triage

The triage guide for axolotl emergencies: how to tell urgent from not-urgent, what to do in the first minutes, what NOT to do, and when to call an exotic vet immediately.

By Farrukh Abdullah · Edited by Ananda Abidin · Updated 2026-08-22 · 538 words
In this guide

  2. First Steps for Any Stressed or Sick Axolotl
  3. Red Flags That Need a Vet Now
  4. What NOT to Do in an Emergency
  5. Emergency Care by Sickness
  6. Aftercare and Prevention

Some axolotl problems require a vet immediately; most do not. This guide ranks how urgent each situation is, gives the first steps to take, and marks the line where you must get help. For a vet near you, start with the exotic vet guide.

Urgent? If the axolotl needs a vet now, find one: exotic vet guide. Everything below stabilises the animal while you get help.

Is It an Emergency? Use This Severity Ladder

The severity ladder has three tiers.

  • Urgent (act now): heavy labored breathing at the surface, visible bleeding or a torn wound, a belly that is hard and bloated, uncontrollable floating, reddening or peeling skin, or a total refusal to eat with these signs. Do a quick water test and get help.
  • Watch closely (same day): curled gills, constant hiding, clamped or receding gills, floating that comes and goes, reduced appetite — test water first; many are a water-quality problem.
  • Monitor (no panic): low activity for a short period, a single bad smell in the tank, or a small nip — fix water and watch.

First Steps for Any Stressed or Sick Axolotl

Four first steps apply to any sick or stressed axolotl.

  1. Test ammonia and nitrite immediately — water chemistry is behind most emergencies; see managing ammonia and nitrate spikes.
  2. Do a partial water change and confirm the temperature is in the safe range (60–68°F) — follow the water-change guide.
  3. Stop feeding until the situation is understood — an impacted or stressed axolotl needs a rest, not food.
  4. Move nothing yet; observe for 30–60 minutes before isolating.

Red Flags That Need a Vet Now

Six signs call for a vet now.

  • Heavy gasping at the surface combined with limp gills.
  • Blood, an open wound, or skin sloughing.
  • Extreme bloating with a rigid body.
  • A hard, swollen belly that is clearly an impaction — impaction symptoms and treatment.
  • Reddening skin that spreads quickly — red leg syndrome.
  • A sudden, severe appetite loss lasting more than 72 hours — refusing to eat.

Find and call an exotic veterinarian — call ahead so they are ready for you.

What NOT to Do in an Emergency

  • Do not shock the animal with a huge or ultra-cold water change.
  • Do not dose "human" or unidentified medications.
  • Do not do a tea bath or salt bath without a diagnosis — the fungus diagnosis guide decides which, if any, treatment.
  • Do not move the axolotl to completely different water without acclimation — see acclimating a new axolotl.
  • Do not raise temperature to "speed up" recovery — axolotls need cool water.

Emergency Care by Sickness

Once you have stabilised the water and identified the condition, follow the right deep guide:

  • Ammonia burns or curled gills: ammonia burns and curled gills.
  • Severe fungus: fungal infections.
  • Floating: why is my axolotl floating.
  • Not eating: refusing to eat.
  • Bleeding or injury: minor scrapes and wounds.

Aftercare and Prevention

Once stable, keep the animal quiet and isolated while it recovers — quarantine tub — and keep ammonia and nitrite at zero. Most medical reminders are husbandry reminders: stable cool temperature, gentle filtration, and a routine/weekly change cycle prevent the overwhelming majority of emergencies.
