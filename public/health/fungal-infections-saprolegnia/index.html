What is axolotl fungus?

Axolotl fungus is a Saprolegnia infection — a freshwater oomycete that colonizes damaged or stressed tissue and grows into visible cotton-like tufts. It's one of the most common health problems in captive axolotls, and it's also one of the more treatable ones, provided you catch it early and fix the water conditions that let it take hold in the first place.

Which treatment fits your case? Mild, early fungus → black tea bath. Widespread or resistant case → salt bath. Confirm the severity here before treating.

Saprolegnia spores exist in almost every freshwater tank. Under normal conditions, an axolotl's slime coat and immune system keep them from establishing. The infection only becomes visible once something has weakened that defense.

What does axolotl fungus look like?

Axolotl fungus looks like small clumps of white or grey cotton attached to the skin, gills, or limbs. It's easy to mistake at first glance for the animal's normal slime coat, but the two are visually distinct once you know what to check.

White cotton growth on skin

The most common presentation is a fuzzy, slightly raised patch, usually starting at a wound site, a limb stump, or an area of prior injury. It spreads outward from that point rather than appearing uniformly across the body.

Gill fungus

Fungus on the gill filaments looks like fine white threads woven through the feathery gill structure. This location is more serious than a skin patch, because the gills are thinner tissue and closer to the animal's respiratory function.

How to tell it apart from healthy slime coat

Healthy slime coat is smooth, translucent, and even across the body. Fungus is opaque, textured, and localized — it reads as a "growth" rather than a coating, and it tends to sit on top of the skin rather than blending into it.

Behavioral symptoms

An axolotl fighting a fungal infection often shows reduced appetite, more time hiding, and general lethargy. These signs alone don't confirm fungus, but combined with visible tufts, they indicate the infection has progressed past the earliest stage.

What causes axolotl fungus?

Axolotl fungus is caused by a combination of elevated water temperature, poor water chemistry, physical injury, and chronic stress — usually more than one factor at once. Saprolegnia establishes when the axolotl's natural defenses are already compromised, so a fungal outbreak is often a downstream symptom of an upstream husbandry problem.

Water temperature above 22°C

Fungal growth accelerates and immune response weakens once water temperature climbs past 22°C. Axolotls are comfortable between 12°C and 20°C, and pushing consistently above that band is one of the single biggest predictors of a fungal outbreak.

Detectable ammonia or nitrite

Any measurable ammonia or nitrite damages the slime coat and gill tissue, creating the entry points Saprolegnia needs. A reading above 0 ppm on either parameter should be treated as a direct contributor, not a coincidence.

Physical injury

Bites from tank mates, scrapes against sharp decor, or rough handling all break the skin barrier. Fungus frequently starts exactly at these injury sites before spreading outward.

Chronic stress

Excessive water flow, too few hides, overcrowding, or frequent handling keep an axolotl's stress hormones elevated, which suppresses immune function over time — even without an acute injury.

Is it fungus or something else?

Fungus is not the only condition that produces white or discolored patches on an axolotl, so confirming it against the alternatives changes what you should do next.

Fungus vs. bacterial infection

Fungus presents as raised, cottony, three-dimensional growth. A bacterial infection like columnaris tends to look flatter, with grey-white patches that can spread rapidly and sometimes carry a foul smell — a detail fungal patches don't share.

Fungus vs. ammonia burn

Ammonia burn shows up as red, inflamed gill or skin tissue rather than white growth, and it correlates directly with a fresh ammonia or nitrite spike on a water test. Fungus can develop secondarily on ammonia-damaged tissue, so both can be present together.

Fungus vs. parasites

External parasites cause the axolotl to scratch against surfaces and can produce small irritated spots, but they don't create the cotton-like texture that defines a fungal tuft. A water test plus close visual inspection under good lighting usually resolves the ambiguity within minutes.

How serious is the infection?

Severity scales with how much surface area is affected and whether the gills are involved, and that scale should directly decide your treatment intensity.

SeverityWhat you seeResponse
MildOne or two small tufts, axolotl still eating and activeTub and correct water conditions
ModerateMultiple patches, or spread reaching the gillsTub plus salt-bath protocol
SevereFungus covering more than 30% of body or gill surface, appetite loss, lethargyEmergency vet

Fungus covering more than 30% of the body or gill surface is an emergency-vet threshold, not a home-treatment case. Left untreated at that stage, Saprolegnia is lethal — it doesn't plateau on its own.

What should you do immediately?

The first action is to test your water and move the axolotl into a clean hospital tub, before starting any medication. Treating fungus while leaving the underlying water problem in place just produces a slower version of the same outcome — the infection returns as soon as you stop treating.

Set up the hospital tub

Use a separate container filled with clean, dechlorinated water, kept at the cool end of the comfort range — around 14–18°C. Wound healing and immune response both work better at these cooler temperatures than at the axolotl's normal tank temperature.

Test water parameters

Check ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, and temperature. If ammonia or nitrite reads above 0 ppm, or temperature sits above 20°C, correct those first — no topical treatment will hold if the animal goes back into the same water.

Perform daily 100% water changes

In the hospital tub, do a full water change once a day using fresh dechlorinated water matched to the tub's temperature. This keeps waste and bacterial load from compounding on top of the fungal infection.

What treatments work for axolotl fungus?

The right treatment depends on severity: mild cases usually resolve with clean cool water alone, while moderate cases need an active antifungal step layered on top.

Tea bath with Indian almond leaves

Adding Indian almond (catappa) leaves to the hospital tub releases tannins into the water, which darken it, reduce visible stress, and provide mild antibacterial support. This is a supportive measure rather than a primary cure, and it pairs well with any of the treatments below.

Salt bath

For moderate cases, a salt bath uses 2–3 teaspoons of salt per litre of dechlorinated water, applied for 10 minutes and never exceeding 15, once or twice a day. Table salt, cooking salt, or iodized salt all work for this short-duration bath — the common belief that only non-iodized salt is safe doesn't hold up against the actual protocol.

Methylene blue

Methylene blue at a low dose is one of the more commonly used antifungal treatments for axolotls, applied as a short bath rather than a permanent tank additive. If you're uncertain about dosing, a vet consult before use is the safer route, since amphibian dosing differs from the fish-medication instructions printed on most bottles.

Which medications are dangerous for axolotls?

Malachite green, copper-based products, and formalin — alone or in combination — are unsafe for axolotls without direct veterinary guidance. Axolotls absorb chemicals through their skin and external gills at a higher rate than fish do, so a dose that's routine for a goldfish tank can be harmful here.

If a product's label is written for fish and doesn't specifically address amphibians, treat that as a reason to check with a vet before use, not a reason to assume it's safe at a lower dose.

How long does axolotl fungus take to heal?

Mild-to-moderate axolotl fungus typically shows visible improvement within 2 to 5 days of starting treatment, with full clearance in 1 to 2 weeks. Severe cases involving significant gill damage take longer and may need veterinary-directed medication throughout.

What recovery looks like day to day

In the first two to three days, expect the cottony texture to start looking thinner or patchier rather than fully clearing — that's the treatment working, not a sign it's failing. By the end of week one, most mild cases show clean tissue at the original site, though full gill regrowth can lag a few days behind skin healing.

Signs treatment isn't working

If the fungus keeps spreading after 3–4 days of consistent tubbing and water changes, or the axolotl stops eating entirely, that's the point to stop waiting and involve a vet rather than extending the same protocol further.

How do you stop fungus from coming back?

Preventing recurrence means keeping the same conditions that caused the first infection from reappearing, since a healed axolotl is not more resistant to Saprolegnia than it was before.

  • Hold temperature at 12–20°C, checked with a reliable thermometer rather than assumed from room temperature.
  • Test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate weekly, and immediately after any sign of stress or illness.
  • Remove sharp decor and address tank-mate aggression — most fungal outbreaks trace back to an injury site.
  • Keep the tank appropriately stocked, since overcrowding raises both physical injury risk and stress hormones.
  • Feed a consistent, appropriate diet to support immune function rather than leaving the axolotl nutritionally stressed.

When should you see an exotic vet?

See an exotic-amphibian vet if fungus covers more than 30% of the body or gill surface, if it spreads despite 3–4 days of consistent home treatment, or if the axolotl stops eating. Not every exotic vet has amphibian experience, so it's worth identifying one in your area before you need one urgently rather than searching during an active infection.

When you contact a vet, have water parameter readings from the last 24 hours, photos of the affected area, and a timeline of when the fungus first appeared ready to share — it shortens the diagnostic process considerably.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can axolotl fungus spread to other axolotls in the same tank?

Yes. Saprolegnia spores are present in the water once an infection is active, so isolating the affected axolotl in a hospital tub reduces the risk to tank mates while treatment is underway.

Is axolotl fungus contagious to humans?

No, Saprolegnia specifically is not a human health risk, though general hand-washing after handling any aquarium animal is still good practice.

Can I treat axolotl fungus without removing the axolotl from the main tank?

It's possible for very mild cases if the whole tank's water quality is corrected, but a hospital tub gives you tighter control over temperature and cleanliness, which speeds recovery in most cases.

Will fungus come back after successful treatment?

Only if the underlying cause — temperature, water chemistry, injury, or stress — isn't addressed. An axolotl that's fully recovered has no lasting vulnerability to reinfection beyond what any axolotl has.
