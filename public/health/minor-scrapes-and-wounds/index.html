Treating Minor Scrapes and Wounds on Axolotls

A small superficial scrape may heal with stable clean water and protection from further trauma, but an amphibian wound should not automatically be treated with salt, tea or over-the-counter medication. Amphibian skin is biologically important and injuries can become infected or be deeper than they first appear.

What should you do after noticing a scrape?

  1. Identify and remove the source of injury if it is safe to do so.
  2. Measure water quality and temperature.
  3. Photograph the area in neutral light.
  4. Reduce unnecessary handling.
  5. Monitor appetite, swimming, swelling, bleeding and whether the lesion is enlarging.

When is a wound more than “minor”?

Seek veterinary care for ongoing bleeding, deep laceration, exposed tissue, fracture, limb loss, substantial swelling, ulceration, spreading discoloration, respiratory difficulty or rapid decline. Merck's amphibian guidance emphasizes rapid assessment, supportive care and pain management for traumatic injuries.

Should you use a tea or salt bath?

No bath should be the default response to a scrape. White/cotton-like material can have several causes and needs its own assessment. Use the fungus guide for that differential rather than treating fuzz from appearance alone.

Should you use an antiseptic or antibiotic?

Do not apply human topical products or start antimicrobial medication without veterinary guidance. Amphibian skin is highly permeable, and drug concentration and route matter.

How do you prevent repeat injuries?

Inspect décor, intake guards, tank mates, handling practices and transport containers. Smooth sharp edges, remove trapping hazards and separate incompatible animals when necessary.
