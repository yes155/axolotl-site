Setting Up an Axolotl Hospital Quarantine Tub

A quarantine or hospital container is a temporary controlled environment, not a treatment by itself. Its purpose is to separate an animal when isolation is needed, simplify observation, and make water quality easy to measure and manage while the underlying health problem is assessed.

When is temporary isolation useful?

Isolation can be appropriate for a new arrival, an injured animal that must be protected from tank mates, an animal being evaluated for infectious disease, or a patient whose veterinarian wants close observation in a simplified setup.

It is not automatically necessary for every missed meal, floating episode, fungal-looking patch or minor scrape.

What should a hospital setup include?

  • a clean, inert container large enough for the animal to rest and turn normally;
  • treated water appropriate for the species;
  • stable temperature;
  • a secure cover when escape is possible;
  • a smooth hide when it does not interfere with observation; and
  • dedicated equipment that is not shared with other tanks.

Does an unfiltered tub need complete daily water changes?

Not as a universal rule. An unfiltered container has no established biofilter, so ammonia can accumulate quickly and water replacement may need to be frequent or complete. The correct schedule depends on container volume, animal size, feeding, waste and measured water quality.

Test the water and change enough to keep conditions safe. If the animal needs prolonged isolation, a properly cycled quarantine aquarium can be easier to stabilize than an indefinitely unfiltered tub.

Should you add salt, tea or medication to the tub?

No product should be added simply because the animal is in quarantine. A hospital container is not a default medication bath. Use medication, saline or another treatment only for a defined indication and preferably under veterinary guidance.

For white/cottony lesions, use the fungus guide. For black tea and salt specifically, use the dedicated evidence pages.

How should you monitor the animal?

Record temperature, water chemistry, appetite, fecal production, posture, breathing, swimming, visible lesions and body condition. Photographs taken from the same angle can help show whether a wound or swelling is changing.

Merck's amphibian clinical guidance emphasizes exactly this kind of history and water-quality record when evaluating an amphibian patient.

How do you prevent cross-contamination?

  • use separate nets, tubs, siphons and feeding tools;
  • wash hands and change gloves between enclosures;
  • do not pour quarantine water into an established aquarium; and
  • clean/disinfect equipment appropriately before reuse.

When should quarantine end?

Do not use a fixed number of days as the only release criterion. The animal should be clinically appropriate to return, the reason for isolation should be resolved or controlled, and the destination aquarium should be safe. For an infectious-disease concern, follow the veterinarian's quarantine period.

Sources

  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Clinical Techniques in Amphibians, updated February 2026
  • Merck Veterinary Manual: Environment and Husbandry for Amphibians
