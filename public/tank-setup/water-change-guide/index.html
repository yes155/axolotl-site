How to Do an Axolotl Water Change Step by Step

A complete step-by-step guide to changing your axolotl's tank water - how often, how much to remove, and how to treat and match replacement water without stressing the animal.

Regular water changes are the most important routine maintenance task in an axolotl tank. Waste breaks down into ammonia and nitrates that even a cycled filter cannot fully remove, and a consistent change schedule keeps toxins low and gills healthy.

How Often Should You Change the Water?

The change schedule has four cases.

  • Cycling tank: daily or every other day for the first 4–6 weeks, until the cycle finishes.
  • Adults in a cycled tank: a 20–25% change once a week.
  • Juveniles: two to three times a week — they eat more relative to their size and add more waste.
  • After a spike: ammonia or nitrite spikes call for a larger or repeated change; see managing ammonia and nitrate spikes.

What You Need Before You Start

You need four things ready before touching the tank: a siphon or bucket, water conditioner, thermometer, and an ammonia test kit. Prepare them all first so the axolotl is disturbed for the shortest possible time.

  • A bucket or siphon reserved for axolotl use only (washed with water, never detergent).
  • Water conditioner — the water-conditioner guide.
  • A thermometer to match temperature.
  • A test kit for ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate.

Step 1: Test the Tank Water

Test ammonia, nitrite, pH, and temperature before changing anything. The test tells you how big a change is actually needed and gives a before-reading to compare after.

Step 2: Prepare and Dechlorinate Replacement Water

Fill your bucket with fresh tap water and treat it with water conditioner at the label dose. The conditioner removes chlorine and chloramine instantly; untreated tap water damages gills and slime coat.

Step 3: Match the Temperature

Bring the replacement water to the same temperature as the tank (60–68°F / 15–20°C). A swing of more than 2–3°F (1–2°C) stresses the axolotl and can trigger floating or illness; see keeping the tank cool.

Step 4: Remove Water from the Tank

Use a siphon or a clean cup, drawing water from the top of the tank while keeping the siphon above the substrate so you neither vacuum up the axolotl nor rearrange clean sand. Never drop the water level below about half the tank in one go.

Step 5: Add Fresh Water Slowly

Pour the replacement water against the glass or a baffle, never directly onto the axolotl. Slow addition keeps temperature and chemistry even and avoids frightening the animal.

Step 6: Test Again and Log the Readings

Retest ammonia and nitrite about an hour later. In a cycled tank the numbers stay unchanged or lower; the nitrogen cycle tracker logs trends between changes.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Four mistakes cause most water-change harm.

  • Skipping the conditioner — tap-water chlorine is a common cause of gill damage.
  • Changing the whole tank at once — massive changes destabilize chemistry.
  • Ignoring the smell — an ammonia-smelling tank needs testing and more frequent changes; see why the tank water stinks.
  • Leaving uneaten food — rotting food becomes this week's ammonia spike; manage uneaten food.

When a Water Change Is Not the Answer

A water change is still the first step for every axolotl, and almost always enough. When the animal looks actively stressed — floating uncontrollably, refusing food, reddened skin, or severe gill damage — the emergency first-aid guide decides whether it needs immediate care.
