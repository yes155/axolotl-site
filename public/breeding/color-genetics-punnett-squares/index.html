How Do Axolotl Color Genes Work?

Axolotl color is controlled by four recessive pigment genes, a (albino), m (melanoid), ax (axanthic), and d (white), inherited independently across 14 pairs of chromosomes. An axolotl has 28 chromosomes in 14 pairs, and the color genes sit on pairs without linkage to each other.

Each gene has two alleles, one from each parent. A dominant allele, written with a capital letter, masks a recessive allele, written lowercase. An animal must carry two copies of a recessive allele, such as m/m, to show that morph.

Three pigment cell types create color. Melanophores produce black-brown pigment, xanthophores produce yellow, and iridophores produce metallic shine. Each pigment gene reduces or removes one or more of these cell types.

The wild type phenotype requires no recessive allele and shows all three pigment cells.

How Do You Read an Axolotl Punnett Square?

A Punnett square places one parent's two alleles along the top and the other parent's two alleles down the side, then combines each pair to show every possible offspring genotype. Each of the four boxes represents one possible genotype at 25% odds.

Build the square for a single gene at a time. For the melanoid gene, a melanoid parent contributes m and m, and a wild type carrier contributes M and m.

The genotype written in each box is the combination of one allele from each parent, and the phenotype shown depends on whether the recessive allele is present twice.

The percentages in the four boxes are per-offspring odds, not a guarantee across a clutch.

What Does a Melanoid Cross Produce?

Crossing a melanoid (m/m) with a wild type carrier (M/m) produces 50% melanoid and 50% wild type offspring. Each offspring has a 1 in 2 chance of receiving m from both parents.

Here is the Punnett square for m/m x M/m:

Mm
mM/mm/m
mM/mm/m

Two wild type carriers (M/m x M/m) produce 25% melanoid, 50% wild type carriers, and 25% wild type with no m allele. Two melanoids (m/m x m/m) produce 100% melanoid.

An axolotl is a melanoid only with the genotype m/m. A single M allele produces a normal wild type appearance.

What Does an Albino Cross Produce?

Crossing an albino (a/a) with a wild type carrier (A/a) produces 50% albino and 50% wild type that carry the albino gene. The a allele is recessive, so two a's are required to remove melanin.

Here is the Punnett square for a/a x A/a:

Aa
aA/aa/a
aA/aa/a

Two albinos (a/a x a/a) produce 100% albino offspring, while two wild type carriers (A/a x A/a) produce 25% albino.

Albinos lack eumelanin, so they appear white or golden with pink or red eyes and gills, depending on which other genes they carry.

How Do You Combine Two Genes?

To predict the color of offspring that differ in two genes, build a 16-box Punnett square using all four allele combinations from each parent. Each of the 16 boxes is one possible genotype at 6.25% odds.

For example, a golden albino (a/a D/+) bred with a leucistic (d/d A/+) needs a cross for both the albino and the white gene. First determine each parent's four possible allele pairs: a/a gives only a, while D/+ gives D or +.

Combine them into a 4 x 4 grid the same way you combine a 2 x 2. Add the boxes with the same phenotype to find the percentage of each color in the clutch.

The white gene (d/d) changes pigment distribution rather than pigment production, which is why d/d with A/+ gives a leucistic and d/d with a/a gives a white albino.

What Genotype Produces Each Morph?

Wild type is D/-, leucistic is d/d, golden albino is a/a with D/-, and melanoid is m/m. Each morph is defined by one homozygous recessive pair plus the dominant alleles at every other locus.

Here is how the core morphs map to genotypes and pigment changes:

Frequently Asked Questions

Axolotls have four main pigment genes, a (albino), m (melanoid), ax (axanthic), and d (white), plus newer mutations still being studied.

A melanoid has no shiny eye ring because the m/m genotype removes iridophores entirely, so the metallic ring around the pupil that other morphs show is absent.

Two wild types produce a leucistic only when both carry the d allele, with 25% odds for a d/d x d/d carrier cross.

A golden albino is a/a with a functional D allele, so it lacks melanophores but keeps the yellow xanthophores.

A wild type genotype is D/- A/- M/- Ax/-, meaning it carries no homozygous recessive color pair.

Limitations of Punnett Squares

Punnett squares predict outcomes at a single locus, which makes them the right tool for the classic diallelic genes (leucistic, albino, melanoid, axanthic). They cannot predict how an animal will look when genetics play out unpredictably: chimeras arise from fused embryos (two separate animals in one), mosaics from a mutation in one embryonic cell line, and GFP and copper intensity vary with polygenic modifiers rather than one gene. For those cases the biology is covered in the pigment-cells guide.

Worked Example: Leucistic x Albino (Two-Gene Cross)

Each gene resolves independently. For the white locus, a leucistic parent (d/d) bred to a wild-color carrier (D/d) gives 50% leucistic and 50% wild colour for that locus. The albino locus works the same way as a separate diallelic recessive. So a single pairing reads as two independent 50/50 flips: roughly a quarter of the clutch can end up showing the albino trait, a quarter leucistic, a quarter leucistic+albino (both recessives), and a quarter visibly wild type — before considering pigment intensity, which Punnett squares do not model.
