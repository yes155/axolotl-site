Earthworms vs. Bloodworms: Which Is Better for Axolotls?

Earthworms are the better staple food for axolotls, because they deliver complete nutrition and a near-ideal calcium-to-phosphorus ratio, while bloodworms fall short on both counts. Bloodworms still earn a place in the tank — just not as the everyday meal.

Are Earthworms and Bloodworms the Same Worm?

Earthworms and bloodworms are not the same organism at all. Earthworms belong to the Annelida phylum — true segmented worms that live in soil. Bloodworms are the larvae of Chironomidae midge flies, an aquatic insect, not a worm in the biological sense. The confusion is understandable: both are red-to-brown, wriggling, worm-shaped feeder animals sold in the same pet-store aisle.

But the mix-up matters for feeding decisions, since an insect larva and a soil annelid have very different nutrient profiles, which is the whole reason this comparison exists.

Earthworms vs. Bloodworms: The Quick Verdict

Earthworms win as the staple food; bloodworms work only as a supplement or treat. The deciding factor is calcium: earthworms carry a 2.5:1 calcium-to-phosphorus ratio, while bloodworms sit below 0.5:1. That single number is why earthworm-fed axolotls grow larger, develop fuller gills, and show fewer long-term health problems than axolotls raised mainly on bloodworms.

The exception is age. Juveniles under 3 inches benefit from bloodworms' fat content for fast early growth, and picky eaters transitioning onto earthworms sometimes need bloodworms as a bridge food. Past the 3-inch mark, earthworms should carry the diet.

With the verdict established, the rest of this article works through why — starting with the nutrition numbers behind it.

What Is the Nutritional Difference Between Earthworms and Bloodworms?

Earthworms contain 54–65% protein (dry weight) and a 2.5:1 calcium-to-phosphorus ratio, while bloodworms contain 53–70% protein and a ratio below 0.5:1. The protein numbers look close on paper, but calcium is where the two part ways. Axolotls need that mineral for skeletal development, and a skewed Ca:P ratio over months is how metabolic bone disease creeps in.

Fat tells a similar story in reverse. Earthworms run 2–8% fat, bloodworms run 6–10%. Amino acid quality adds another layer: earthworm protein supplies roughly 373 mg of essential amino acids per gram of protein, with leucine and lysine — both critical for tissue repair — present at 81 mg/g and 71 mg/g respectively. Bloodworms supply all the essential amino acids too, just in lower concentrations per gram.

Moisture content runs close between the two (78–85% for earthworms, roughly 80% for bloodworms fresh), so hydration isn't a differentiator. Ash content is where they diverge again — earthworms range 10.8–35.2%, bloodworms sit lower at 5.0–8.0%. Vitamin coverage splits by type: earthworms carry B-complex vitamins and vitamin C, while bloodworms carry B1, B6, and B12, plus a genuine advantage in dietary iron, thanks to the hemoglobin that gives them their red color.

Nutrition on paper only matters if the axolotl can actually break it down — which is a question of digestibility, not just composition.

Are Earthworms More Digestible Than Bloodworms?

Earthworms are more digestible than bloodworms, because earthworms carry negligible chitin while bloodworms have a thin chitinous cuticle. Axolotls produce chitinase and break bloodworms down without trouble, if the worms are properly thawed before feeding. Skip that step — drop a freeze-dried cube straight into the tank dry — and the worm can expand in the gut, raising impaction risk.

Earthworms don't carry that caveat at all. Their soft, moist bodies hydrolyze into free amino acids with minimal digestive effort, which is part of why keepers lean on them for animals recovering from illness or gut upset.

Digestibility feeds directly into growth outcomes — a worm the axolotl can't efficiently break down doesn't translate its nutrition into size or condition.

Which Worm Supports Better Axolotl Growth and Health?

Earthworm-based diets produce larger adult size, fuller gills, and fewer health problems than bloodworm-based diets. The mechanism traces in a straight line: a 2.5:1 Ca:P ratio supports proper bone mineralization, which drives correct skeletal development, which in turn supports healthy limb and tail regeneration — an axolotl signature trait — and structural soundness that holds up over years, not just months.

Bloodworm-only diets break that chain at the first link. Without adequate calcium, mineralization lags, and axolotls raised primarily on bloodworms tend to plateau at a smaller adult size with a higher rate of nutritional deficiency.

The fat difference matters on a separate axis. Bloodworms' higher fat content raises the odds of obesity and fatty liver disease in adults if they become a daily habit rather than an occasional extra. None of this rules bloodworms out — it just points to where they fit: juveniles building mass fast, or a treat folded into an earthworm-based rotation.

Health outcomes inside the tank connect to what happens in the water around the axolotl, too — which is where feeding choice starts affecting maintenance.

How Do Earthworms and Bloodworms Affect Water Quality?

Bloodworms foul tank water faster than earthworms, because uneaten bloodworms decompose within 10–15 minutes while earthworm scraps stay intact for up to 30 minutes. Axolotls tend to swallow earthworms whole or in clean bites, leaving little behind. Bloodworms fall apart in the water column instead, and once they do, they release ammonia and organic waste fast enough to spike toxicity in an established tank.

The fix is mechanical, not dietary: feed with tongs or a dish so you can see exactly what's uneaten, then pull it before the clock runs out. A 10–20% weekly water change and routine ammonia checks cover the rest.

Water quality risk is one side of the safety picture. The other side is what's inside the worm before it ever reaches the tank.

Are Earthworms or Bloodworms Safer From Parasites and Contaminants?

Both earthworms and bloodworms carry contamination risk, but the risks differ in kind. Earthworms are soil bioaccumulators — they can concentrate pesticide residue and heavy metals like lead or cadmium if sourced from contaminated ground. Bloodworms, being aquatic, can carry waterborne pathogens such as Aeromonas hydrophila, along with intestinal parasites, particularly in live form.

Supplier selection is the actual control point for both risks. Reputable earthworm suppliers test soil stock for pesticide and heavy-metal residue before selling, and commercially farmed or home-cultured worms carry far less risk than anything wild-caught from unknown ground. Reputable bloodworm suppliers test their water sources and sell frozen rather than live stock, since freezing cuts live-pathogen exposure substantially.

Quarantining any new live food for a few days before it reaches the main tank adds a second layer of protection either way.

Sourcing safely is one cost. The dollar cost is the other — and it tends to be the deciding factor for keepers choosing a staple.

Which Is Cheaper: Earthworms or Bloodworms?

Earthworms cost $5–$30 per month as a staple food, while bloodworms cost $6–$12 per month for a frozen pack that supplies less complete nutrition per dollar. The earthworm range spans budget bait-shop cups up to bulk or home-cultured supply — and a home vermiculture bin pushes the ongoing cost toward zero after the initial setup, since the worms breed and replenish themselves.

Storage cost factors into the total picture too. Earthworms need only a bedding box held at 38–45°F, which costs nothing beyond the box itself. Bloodworms need dedicated freezer space held at 0°F or below, and since bloodworms can't be practically home-cultured the way earthworms can, that cost recurs with every single purchase rather than trending toward zero over time.

Bloodworms sit in a narrower price band because they're bought in fixed frozen packs, not scaled by bulk the same way. Cheaper per pack doesn't mean cheaper per axolotl, though: since bloodworms can't carry a diet alone, most owners end up buying both anyway.

Cost and nutrition both shift with the axolotl's age — which is where the feeding schedule comes in.

How Should You Feed Earthworms and Bloodworms by Axolotl Age?

Feeding frequency and worm choice both shift with axolotl size, moving from multiple daily feedings for hatchlings down to two or three feedings a week for adults.

Bloodworms carry more weight early on — juveniles handle small portions easily, and the extra fat supports fast growth. As the axolotl crosses the 3-inch mark, earthworms should take over as the backbone, with bloodworms sliding into an occasional-treat role rather than disappearing outright.

Age is the main variable, but it's not the only one. Some situations call for a different approach entirely.

What Should You Feed in Special Situations?

Feeding strategy should shift for picky eaters, recovering axolotls, obese axolotls, bloodworm-only diets, and underweight axolotls — each situation has a different fix.

  • Picky eater refusing earthworms. Red wigglers secrete a bitter substance when stressed, which puts off sensitive eaters. Blanching the worm for about three seconds in boiling water, then cooling it immediately, removes the taste. If refusal continues, offering bloodworms briefly to re-establish appetite, then reintroducing earthworms gradually, works better than forcing the switch outright.
  • Recovering axolotl (post-illness or post-injury). Earthworms are the better choice here, since their low chitin content and high digestibility reduce digestive strain during recovery. Blackworms are a reasonable alternative for the same reason.
  • Obese axolotl. Cut bloodworms back to an occasional treat rather than a regular feeding, since their fat content is the main driver of excess weight. Adding a fasting day or two per week, alongside earthworms as the primary food, helps bring body condition back down gradually.
  • Bloodworm-only diet (already established). This diet carries real deficiency risk, particularly for calcium, if it continues long-term. Transition toward earthworms gradually rather than switching abruptly — abrupt diet changes are the most common cause of feeding refusal in axolotls.
  • Underweight axolotl. Increase feeding frequency and add bloodworms temporarily for their calorie density, paired with earthworms for the calcium and protein completeness a recovery diet needs. Gut-loading earthworms with a nutritious feed for 24 hours before offering them boosts the nutritional payoff further.

Whichever situation applies, preparation and storage habits matter just as much as the worm choice itself.

How Do You Prepare and Store Earthworms vs. Bloodworms?

Earthworms stay fresh for 2–4 weeks refrigerated, while frozen bloodworms keep for 6–12 months and freeze-dried bloodworms last 2–3 years. Storage tips the convenience scale toward bloodworms, but preparation tips it back.

Rinse earthworms in cool water and cut them to roughly head-width before feeding. Species matters here: European Nightcrawlers and Canadian Nightcrawlers are large-bodied and suit adult axolotls well, red wigglers suit smaller axolotls but carry the bitter-secretion issue above, and composting worms or ordinary fishing-shop worms work as a budget substitute for either.

Red wigglers specifically benefit from the three-second blanch described earlier if an axolotl is turning its nose up at them.

Bloodworms need the opposite care: thaw frozen bloodworm cubes in tank water for 5–10 minutes, then deliver with a pipette or tongs rather than dumping the whole cube in, and skip feeding them straight from the freezer.

All of this — nutrition, digestibility, growth outcomes, water quality, safety, cost, age-based feeding, and special situations — points to the same conclusion.

Conclusion: Building an Axolotl Diet Around Earthworms

Earthworms belong at the center of an axolotl's diet, with bloodworms in a supporting role. The gap comes down to one number: a 2.5:1 Ca:P ratio versus one below 0.5:1 is the difference between a diet that builds a skeleton correctly over years and one that slowly undermines it.

By life stage, that means baby brine shrimp and daphnia for hatchlings, a chopped earthworm-and-bloodworm mix for juveniles building size fast, and whole earthworms as the backbone from 3 inches onward — with bloodworms folded in two or three times a week as a treat, never as the everyday meal.

Safety comes down to sourcing: reputable, tested suppliers for both worm types, frozen over live where possible, and prompt removal of uneaten food to protect water quality. Get those pieces right, and the nutrition numbers above translate into an axolotl that grows to full size, holds good body condition, and stays healthy for the long run.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can axolotls eat bloodworms every day?

Axolotls can eat bloodworms daily as juveniles, but adults should not eat bloodworms daily, because the fat content raises the risk of obesity and fatty liver disease over time. Two to three servings a week, alongside earthworms, keeps the treat in proportion.

Do red wigglers taste bad to axolotls?

Red wigglers taste bitter to axolotls when the worm is stressed, since it secretes a defensive substance in that state. Blanching the worm for about three seconds in boiling water removes the taste before feeding.

How many earthworms should I feed my adult axolotl?

Adult axolotls should eat 1–3 whole earthworms per feeding, offered two to three times a week. Juveniles under 3 inches need the worms chopped into smaller pieces instead of fed whole.

Is it safe to feed axolotls wild-caught earthworms?

Wild-caught earthworms carry meaningfully more risk than commercially farmed ones, because soil worms bioaccumulate pesticides and heavy metals from contaminated ground. Commercially farmed or home-cultured earthworms avoid that risk almost entirely.

Can axolotls survive on a bloodworms-only diet?

Axolotls can survive short-term on a bloodworms-only diet, but the diet causes real problems long-term, since bloodworms lack sufficient calcium for proper skeletal development. A bloodworm-only diet should transition toward earthworms as the staple within weeks, not months.

What's the actual difference between earthworms and bloodworms?

Earthworms are terrestrial Annelids with complete nutrition and a 2.5:1 Ca:P ratio, while bloodworms are aquatic midge fly larvae with a Ca:P ratio below 0.5:1. The practical difference is that earthworms work as a staple and bloodworms work only as a supplement.

Why does my axolotl refuse to eat earthworms?

Axolotls refuse earthworms most often due to stress, an abrupt diet switch, or worm size mismatched to the axolotl's mouth. Cutting the worm smaller, blanching red wigglers to remove bitterness, and introducing earthworms gradually alongside a familiar food usually resolves it within a week or two.

How often should axolotls be fed overall, regardless of worm type?

Hatchlings should be fed 2–3 times daily, juveniles once daily, and adults two to three times a week, regardless of which worm makes up the meal. Fasting days for adults are healthy and mimic natural feeding patterns in the wild.
