What Do Axolotls Eat?

Axolotls eat live and frozen meat-based prey, including earthworms, blackworms, bloodworms, brine shrimp, and sinking carnivore pellets. Axolotls are obligate carnivores, meaning their digestive system processes only animal protein and cannot extract nutrition from plant matter. They hunt by suction feeding: the axolotl opens its mouth and inhales water along with the prey item, then swallows it whole.

A captive diet built around earthworms and high-protein sinking pellets replicates the nutrient profile axolotls evolved to need — and that diet, in turn, shapes everything downstream of it, from how the axolotl digests its food to how clean its tank water stays.

What Do Axolotls Eat in the Wild?

Wild axolotls eat insects, worms, crustaceans, snails, tadpoles, and small fish in the lake systems of Xochimilco, Mexico City. Native axolotls hunt by detecting water movement through their lateral line system, then vacuuming nearby prey into their mouths rather than chasing it down. Prey availability shifts with the season: insect larvae and crustaceans dominate during warmer months, while mollusks and detritus-feeding invertebrates make up more of the diet when insect populations drop.

This opportunistic pattern explains why captive axolotls accept such a wide range of foods — their digestive system never specialized around a single prey type.

What Are an Axolotl's Nutritional Requirements?

An axolotl's diet needs to fall between 30% and 60% protein by dry weight, with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio above 1:1 and fat kept low. Protein drives muscle and tissue growth, which is why juveniles — who are growing fastest — need the higher end of that range.

Calcium supports skeletal development and gill cartilage, while excess fat is linked to fatty liver disease, one of the more common causes of preventable mortality in captive axolotls. Moisture content matters too: axolotls evolved to eat prey that's mostly water by weight, so dry, low-moisture foods like crickets digest poorly by comparison.

Getting these nutritional ratios right is the foundation everything else in this guide builds on — the food choices, the feeding schedule, and the water-quality routine downstream of digestion all trace back to this single requirement.

What Are the Best Foods for Axolotls?

Earthworms (Nightcrawlers, Red Wigglers, Dendrobaena)

Earthworms are the single most complete food for an axolotl over 3 inches long. Earthworms contain over 60% protein with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio greater than 1, a soft body wall with no indigestible shell, and bioavailable nutrients in their natural form. Feed earthworms whole to adults, or cut into thirds for juveniles, as often as daily for fast-growing juveniles and every 2–3 days for adults.

The main risk is sourcing worms from soil treated with pesticides or fertilizer, so only feed worms from a trusted bait shop or your own pesticide-free compost.

Blackworms

Blackworms are the best transitional food for hatchlings once they start growing legs but are still under 3 inches long. They're small enough for an undeveloped mouth to swallow whole and nutritionally complete enough to support that early growth spurt on their own, fed in small amounts multiple times a day.

Bloodworms

Bloodworms work as an occasional supplement, not a staple — bloodworms lack the long-term nutritional density earthworms provide, particularly in calcium. Feeding bloodworms as the sole diet over weeks or months leads to poor growth and weakened bone development, so they're best rotated in once or twice a week alongside earthworms or pellets.

Brine Shrimp and Daphnia

Brine shrimp and daphnia suit hatchlings before their legs develop, since both are small enough to be inhaled by an undeveloped mouth and easy to digest. Beyond the hatchling stage, they're too low in mass to serve as a primary food and work better as an occasional treat.

Axolotl and Salmon Pellets

Sinking carnivore pellets — including axolotl-specific formulas and salmon pellets — should contain at least 40% protein and under 10% fat to count as a suitable supplement. Soft pellets digest more easily than hard ones; hard pellets can be soaked in water briefly to soften before feeding.

Pellets work well as a convenient supplement to a worm-based diet starting once a juvenile reaches roughly 1.5–2 inches in length, fed at a rate of about 1–2 pellets per inch of body length, but they shouldn't fully replace live or frozen food, since they fall short of earthworms on bioavailable calcium.

What Foods Should Axolotls Avoid?

Axolotls should avoid feeder fish, mealworms, superworms, waxworms, crickets, and any land-based meat like beef, chicken, or processed deli meat. Feeder fish carry a meaningful risk of introducing parasites and disease into a tank, since most feeder stock is raised in crowded, low-quality conditions.

Mealworms and superworms have a chitin exoskeleton that axolotls can't digest, which raises the risk of intestinal impaction — the same blockage risk discussed below under digestion and impaction. Crickets pose the same chitin problem. Beef, chicken, and dairy products like cheese contain fat ratios and protein structures suited to a mammal's digestive system, not an amphibian's, and routinely cause digestive distress.

Bread, fruit, and vegetables offer essentially no usable nutrition to an obligate carnivore and displace foods the axolotl actually needs.

Live Food vs. Frozen Food vs. Pellets: Which Is Best?

The best axolotl diet combines all three formats, since each one solves a different problem the others don't. Live food provides the highest nutrient bioavailability and triggers natural hunting behavior, but carries the highest disease and parasite risk if sourced from low-quality stock and is the most labor-intensive to store.

Frozen food — bloodworms and brine shrimp in particular — kills off most parasites during freezing, costs less to store long-term, but loses some nutrient density in the freeze-thaw process and lacks the hunting stimulation live prey provides.

Pellets are the most convenient and shelf-stable option and let an owner control protein and fat content precisely, but most axolotls take longer to recognize them as food, and pellets alone fall short on the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio earthworms naturally provide.

How Should You Prepare Axolotl Food?

Axolotl food preparation means cutting prey to a size no wider than the gap between the axolotl's eyes, thawing frozen food fully before feeding, and removing uneaten portions within 15–20 minutes. Live earthworms need rinsing under cool water to remove loose soil before going into the tank, since soil residue adds unnecessary waste load to the water.

Frozen bloodworms or brine shrimp should thaw in a small cup of tank water rather than going in frozen, since a cold food mass dropped straight into the tank can shock a cool-water animal already living at 60–68°F (15–20°C). Pellets that arrive hard benefit from a short soak in tank water to soften them, since soft food reduces the impaction risk covered in the digestion section below.

What Are Safe Feeding Practices for Axolotls?

Safe axolotl feeding means using feeding tongs or a turkey baster rather than bare hands, target-feeding at the bottom of the tank rather than the surface, and removing leftovers promptly. Tongs let the keeper control portion placement and avoid the axolotl mistaking a finger for food, since axolotls have poor eyesight and rely on the suction-feeding reflex described earlier.

Feeding at the surface increases the chance the axolotl gulps air along with the food, which is one of the more common causes of the temporary floating discussed under feeding problems below. Removing uneaten food matters because decomposing protein is the single largest contributor to ammonia buildup — the bridge between feeding habits and water quality covered next.

How Does Diet Affect an Axolotl's Digestion?

Diet affects digestion directly: soft, high-moisture foods like earthworms pass through an axolotl's gut easily, while hard-shelled or oversized items risk lodging in the digestive tract as impaction. Impaction is a physical blockage, most often caused by swallowed gravel substrate, chitin-shelled prey like mealworms, or food pieces cut too large for the axolotl's mouth gap.

A blockage prevents both digestion and waste passage, which is why impaction prevention starts upstream, at the food-selection and food-prep stages already covered above, not after symptoms appear.

How Does Digestion Connect to Tank Waste?

Everything an axolotl digests eventually becomes waste, and everything it doesn't eat decomposes in the tank as organic matter — both processes generate ammonia. An axolotl that's overfed produces more waste than its biofilter can process at once, and any food left uneaten breaks down on the tank bottom through the same bacterial process, adding a second, avoidable ammonia source on top of normal metabolic waste.

This is the direct mechanical link between feeding habits and the water-quality parameters covered next: portion control and prompt leftover removal aren't just digestion concerns, they're water-quality concerns.

How Does Diet Affect Water Quality?

Diet affects water quality primarily through ammonia: uneaten food and excess waste decompose into ammonia, which is toxic to axolotls at concentrations as low as 1 ppm. Ammonia and nitrite should both read 0 ppm in a healthy, cycled tank, while nitrate should stay below roughly 20–40 ppm depending on the source consulted, with most keepers targeting the lower end of that range.

Overfeeding is one of the most common — and most preventable — causes of an ammonia spike, since it adds both more digestive waste and more uneaten organic matter to the bioload at once.

A 20% weekly water change, combined with siphoning uneaten food and waste off the tank bottom, keeps these parameters stable between cycles.

How Does Water Quality Affect Axolotl Health?

Poor water quality — specifically ammonia or nitrite above 0 ppm — directly damages an axolotl's gills and skin and suppresses its immune response, making diet-related digestive issues worse, not just water issues worse. An axolotl already stressed by elevated ammonia is more likely to refuse food, which compounds into the poor-nutrition symptoms covered later in this guide.

This closes the loop the brief opened with nutrition: what an axolotl eats determines its waste output, waste output determines water chemistry, and water chemistry determines whether the animal can digest and absorb nutrients from its next meal at all. Feeding by Life Stage

Hatchlings (Under 3 Inches)

Hatchlings need feeding multiple times per day on baby brine shrimp, daphnia, or microworms until their legs fully develop, then a transition to blackworms once they're large enough to handle them. Their metabolism runs unusually fast relative to body size, so skipped feedings affect a hatchling's growth far more noticeably than they affect an adult's.

Juveniles (3–6 Inches)

Juveniles need feeding once or twice daily on chopped earthworms, blackworms, and a growing share of soft pellets as they approach 1.5–2 inches. Cut food into thirds for an axolotl this size, since their mouth gap is still small relative to a full nightcrawler, and reduce portion size rather than frequency if you notice the tail base thinning between meals.

Subadults (6–9 Inches)

Subadults need feeding 3–4 times per week on whole earthworms and pellets, with portions scaled up as growth slows from the juvenile rate. This stage is where overfeeding mistakes become more common, since keepers often carry juvenile-stage frequency forward without adjusting for the animal's slowing metabolism.

Adults (9+ Inches)

Adults thrive on just 2–3 feedings per week, typically 1–3 whole earthworms or 5–12 pellets per feeding depending on body size. An adult that's still being fed at juvenile frequency is the single most common cause of axolotl obesity, since their growth-driven calorie need has already leveled off.

How Much Food Should an Axolotl Eat per Feeding?

An axolotl should receive an amount of food it can fully consume within 3–5 minutes. For an adult, that typically means 1–3 earthworms or roughly 5–12 pellets per feeding, scaled to the axolotl's size. Body condition is the better long-term gauge than any fixed number: a healthy axolotl stays thick at the base of the tail without looking bloated, and portion size should shrink the moment that base starts thinning or swelling.

What Are the Signs of Proper Nutrition in an Axolotl?

Signs of proper nutrition include a thick, even tail base, fluffy and undamaged gill filaments, steady growth between size checks, and consistent waste production a day or two after feeding. A well-nourished axolotl also shows a strong, fast feeding response — striking at food immediately rather than ignoring it — since appetite itself is one of the more reliable day-to-day health indicators between vet checks.

What Are the Signs of Poor Nutrition in an Axolotl?

Signs of poor nutrition in an axolotl include a thinning tail base, curled or shrinking gill filaments, lethargy, and refusal to eat. A thin tail base signals the axolotl is metabolizing its own fat reserves faster than diet replaces them, usually from underfeeding or a diet too low in protein.

Curled gills point toward poor water quality or nutritional deficiency rather than diet alone, so check ammonia and nitrate levels alongside feeding habits before assuming the food is the only problem — another instance of the diet-to-water-quality bridge covered above.

What Are Common Axolotl Feeding Mistakes?

Common feeding mistakes include overfeeding, offering prey too large to swallow safely, relying on bloodworms as a staple, and leaving uneaten food in the tank. Oversized prey raises the risk of impaction, since axolotls swallow food whole rather than chewing it down to size — the general rule is that no food item should be wider than the space between the axolotl's eyes.

Leftover food breaks down into ammonia, which is toxic to axolotls at low concentrations, so uneaten food needs removal within 15–20 minutes of feeding.

What Are Common Axolotl Feeding Problems?

Common feeding-related problems include food refusal, involuntary floating, constipation, and impaction, and the four often present with overlapping symptoms that require checking different causes to tell apart. Food refusal most often traces back to poor water quality, a recent large meal still digesting, or temperature outside the 60–68°F range — check ammonia and temperature before assuming illness.

Involuntary floating after a meal is usually mild and caused by swallowed air during surface feeding, resolving within hours on its own; floating that persists for more than a day, especially paired with a swollen abdomen, points toward impaction instead. Constipation shows up as a swollen cloaca and several days without waste, typically from overfeeding or hard-to-digest food, and often resolves with a 24–48 hour fast.

Impaction is the most serious of the four — a physical blockage from swallowed substrate or oversized food that won't resolve with fasting alone and needs prompt intervention, including a substrate check and, if symptoms persist past 48 hours, veterinary care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can axolotls eat fish?

Axolotls can eat small fish occasionally, but fish raised as feeder stock carry a meaningful disease and parasite risk, so most keepers limit fish to rare treats from a trusted source rather than a regular food.

Can axolotls eat shrimp?

Axolotls can eat brine shrimp and mysis shrimp as a supplement, particularly at the hatchling stage, though shrimp alone don't supply enough mass to serve as a primary adult food.

Can axolotls eat pellets every day?

Axolotls can eat pellets daily as part of a mixed diet, provided the pellets supply at least 40% protein and the axolotl also receives earthworms or another whole-prey food regularly to cover the calcium pellets fall short on.

Can axolotls eat mealworms or crickets?

Axolotls should not eat mealworms or crickets as a regular food, because the chitin exoskeleton in both is indigestible and raises the risk of intestinal impaction.

How long can an axolotl go without food?

A healthy adult axolotl can go up to two weeks without food with no lasting harm, since its metabolism slows significantly at typical tank temperatures, though juveniles tolerate fasting periods far less well due to their faster growth-driven metabolism.

Why is my axolotl refusing food?

An axolotl most often refuses food because of elevated ammonia or nitrite, a water temperature above 68°F, or a recently digested large meal — checking water parameters is the first step before assuming illness.

Can axolotls eat frozen food straight from the freezer?

Axolotls should not eat food straight from the freezer; frozen bloodworms or brine shrimp need full thawing in a small cup of tank water first, since a cold food mass can shock the axolotl's temperature-sensitive system.

How much should I feed a juvenile axolotl?

A juvenile axolotl should receive 1–2 chopped earthworms or an equivalent portion of pellets once or twice daily, scaled down in size so each piece is no wider than the gap between its eyes.

Can axolotls overeat?

Axolotls can overeat, and doing so regularly leads to obesity and a higher risk of constipation, since their feeding response doesn't reliably signal fullness the way it does in many other animals.

What does it mean if my axolotl is floating after eating?

Floating shortly after eating usually means the axolotl swallowed air during a surface feeding and will resolve within hours; floating that continues past a day, especially with a swollen belly, points toward impaction instead.
